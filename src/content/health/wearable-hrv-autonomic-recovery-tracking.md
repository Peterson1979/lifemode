## The practical answer

Heart-rate variability is the changing time between beats. A watch can estimate it, but the number changes with sleep, illness, alcohol, stress, training, breathing, and device fit. Compare your own trend under similar conditions; do not compare it with another person’s score.

## A useful routine

Let the same device measure during sleep or a consistent quiet period. Look at a seven-day trend and check how you actually feel. If HRV is lower for several days and you feel run down, choose easy movement, reduce hard intervals, eat normally, and prioritise sleep. If the number is low but you feel well, there is no need to cancel your life.

Consumer sensors can lose accuracy during movement. HRV cannot diagnose anxiety, overtraining, heart disease, or “autonomic imbalance,” and should not change prescribed treatment. Seek urgent care for chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, or a sustained irregular heartbeat.


## What Heart Rate Variability Actually Measures

Contrary to common assumption, a healthy heart does not tick like a metronome with exact, uniform intervals between beats. If your resting pulse is 60 beats per minute, the time elapsed between individual heartbeats might vary from 0.85 seconds to 1.15 seconds.

This beat-to-beat variation in time (the R-R interval on an electrocardiogram) is known as **Heart Rate Variability (HRV)**. It directly reflects the constant push-and-pull between the two branches of your autonomic nervous system (ANS):

- **Sympathetic Nervous System (SNS):** The "fight-or-flight" accelerator, which increases heart rate and makes inter-beat intervals more rigid and uniform (lowering HRV).
- **Parasympathetic Nervous System (PNS):** The "rest-and-digest" brake mediated by the vagus nerve, which slows heart rate and introduces natural breathing-synchronized fluctuations (elevating HRV).

## Decoding the Metrics: RMSSD vs. SDNN

Most commercial wellness devices (such as Oura Ring, Whoop, Apple Watch, and Garmin) record HRV using photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors:

### 1. RMSSD (Root Mean Square of Successive Differences)
Calculates the variance between adjacent heartbeats. RMSSD is the gold standard for tracking **short-term parasympathetic activity** and is the primary mathematical foundation behind most daily "recovery scores" or "readiness indexes."

### 2. SDNN (Standard Deviation of NN Intervals)
Measures overall variability across a full 24-hour recording period. Reflects both sympathetic and parasympathetic input, along with circadian temperature and endocrine cycles.

## The Golden Rule of HRV: Compare Only to Your Own Baseline

A common mistake among wearable users is comparing raw HRV values to peers or social media benchmarks. Absolute HRV is heavily influenced by genetics, left ventricular volume, biological age, and sex:

- An endurance athlete might have an average baseline RMSSD of 110 ms.
- A healthy executive might thrive with a consistent baseline RMSSD of 42 ms.

**What matters is deviation from your personalized 7-day and 30-day rolling baseline:**

| HRV Trend | Physiological Implication | Recommended Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Within Baseline (± 0.5 SD)** | Balanced autonomic equilibrium; high systemic resilience. | Proceed with planned training, high cognitive output, and normal workload. |
| **Depressed (> 1.0 SD Below)** | Sympathetic dominance: acute inflammation, illness, alcohol, poor sleep, or emotional strain. | Prioritize restorative protocols: low-intensity Zone 2 cardio, early bedtime, hydration. |
| **Abnormally Elevated (> 2.0 SD Above)** | Potential parasympathetic saturation: acute overreaching or systemic exhaustion where the body forcibly dampens output. | Do not automatically treat this as high recovery. Observe subjective energy and resting heart rate. |

## Habits Proven to Raise Long-Term HRV Baseline

1. **Eliminate Late Alcohol Consumption:** Even 1–2 drinks within 3 hours of sleep can depress nocturnal HRV by 20% to 50% and elevate nocturnal resting heart rate by 8–12 bpm.
2. **Coherent Resonance Breathing:** Breathing at approximately 5.5 to 6 breaths per minute (inhale 5 seconds, exhale 5 seconds) aligns respiration with baroreflex oscillations, immediately activating parasympathetic tone.
3. **Consistent Aerobic Volume:** Consistent low-intensity aerobic training expands cardiac stroke volume, structurally raising resting parasympathetic tone over months and years.
